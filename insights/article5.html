The Great Bourbon Correction: 16 Million Barrels and the End of Easy Money

In January, a 41-year-old collector named Brian Mosoff raised his paddle in a Sotheby's auction room in New York and paid $162,500 for a single bottle of bourbon — an Old Rip Van Winkle 20-Year Single Barrel, the most expensive bottle of American whiskey ever sold. That same winter, a few hundred miles west in Kentucky, used bourbon barrels that fetched more than $200 apiece a year earlier were going for around $50.

5 min read · Prime Domain Capital

That is the bourbon market in 2026, in two images: a record at the top, and a fire sale at the bottom. The industry overproduced, over-promised, and over-allocated for the better part of a decade, and now the bill has come due. According to the Kentucky Distillers' Association, there are now 16.1 million barrels of bourbon and other spirits aging in Kentucky — an all-time record. Every single one of those barrels gets taxed. The barrel tax hit $75 million in 2025, up 27 percent year over year. Distillers barreled approximately 2.7 million barrels in 2022 alone, and the warehouses have been full ever since.

The production cuts are the most dramatic the industry has ever made. Jim Beam shut down its flagship James B. Beam campus in Clermont, Kentucky, for the entirety of 2026 — when the largest bourbon producer on the planet mothballs its flagship distillery for an entire calendar year, that is not a blip. Heaven Hill trimmed production across its operations in 2025. So did Maker's Mark. So did Wild Turkey. U.S. whiskey output dropped 28 percent through August 2025 compared with the prior year. MGP Ingredients announced in April 2026 that it would temporarily idle distilling operations at two Kentucky facilities, Limestone Branch in Lebanon and Lux Row in Bardstown, effective May 1, affecting 33 employees.

Demand fell at the same time

The slowdown would be manageable on its own. It arrived alongside a demand shock. According to Gallup, just 54 percent of U.S. adults say they drink alcohol — the lowest share in nearly 90 years. American whiskey price and mix swung from plus 0.3 percent in January to roughly minus 5 percent by spring: discounting, not premiums. Nielsen data compiled by Jefferies showed the turn clearly, and Citi's read on Brown-Forman — maker of Jack Daniel's, Woodford Reserve, and Old Forester — showed the company holding volume up only by cutting price hard, with price and mix at minus 3.8 percent in February and minus 4.9 percent in March.

The wholesale data is worse. WSWA's SipSource reported that in the first quarter, core spirits volume fell 4.4 percent while revenue fell 5.7 percent — the widest gap between volume and revenue the service has ever recorded. Bottles priced $50 to $100 fell 8.8 percent, and bottles over $100 fell 9.3 percent. IWSR's full-year 2025 data found global spirits value fell 9 percent. Total U.S. spirits sales fell 1.1 percent in 2024 to $37.2 billion, the first annual revenue decline the Distilled Spirits Council had recorded, then fell 2.2 percent in 2025 to $36.4 billion.

Trade made it worse. EU retaliatory tariffs dropped American whiskey exports 27 percent in recent years, and a temporary suspension of those tariffs expired in February 2026 with no new deal in place. Canadian provinces pulled American spirits from government liquor shelves, driving an estimated 60 to 85 percent drop in U.S. spirits exports to Canada. Brown-Forman saw 2025 net sales fall 5 percent to $4.0 billion, and its Canadian sales plunged 61 percent. Uncle Nearest filed for bankruptcy. Brown-Forman cut 12 percent of staff and fielded a $15 billion takeover approach.

The counterargument: the top is booming, and the gap is the opportunity

None of this means bourbon is dying. It means bourbon is sorting itself out. The $162,500 Pappy is not an anomaly; it is the market's verdict on what is genuinely scarce. Cornerstone bottles like Pappy Van Winkle, William Larue Weller, and George T. Stagg maintain strong demand while mid-tier limited releases see significant volatility. The days of flipping any allocated bottle for a 400 percent markup are essentially over. The market is separating the truly desirable from the merely scarce, and both from the genuinely excellent.

There is a second, quieter argument for the patient buyer. Production cuts in 2025 and 2026 mean fewer barrels entering rickhouses right now, and the whiskey that Jim Beam, Heaven Hill, Wild Turkey, and Maker's Mark are not producing today will not create a supply gap until 2030 at the earliest — and more likely 2033 to 2038 for the aged expressions that drinkers care most about. The pricing window is now: eight-to-twelve-year bourbon that commanded $70 to $90 during peak hype is showing up at $55 to $70, and core bottles in the $30 to $50 range are getting renewed retail attention because distillers need volume, not limited-edition launches.

What the correction means for values

For the collector, the lesson is to stop buying scarcity and start buying quality. The allocated bottle sitting on the shelf at retail is the market telling you it was never scarce, only hyped. For the drinker, 2026 is the best buyer's market since before the boom started: shelves are full, prices are softening, and the whiskey in the glass is, on average, older and better than it was five years ago, because the glut is aged stock, not young stock.

For the investor, the lesson is narrower. The top of the market — the genuinely rare, the genuinely old, the genuinely limited — still appreciates, as the Sotheby's record proves. The middle, where the money was made in 2021 and 2022, is where the correction lives. A bottle bought to flip is a bet on hype. A bottle bought to drink, poured neat or over a single large clear cube, is a bet on the whiskey. In 2026, only one of those bets is still paying.
